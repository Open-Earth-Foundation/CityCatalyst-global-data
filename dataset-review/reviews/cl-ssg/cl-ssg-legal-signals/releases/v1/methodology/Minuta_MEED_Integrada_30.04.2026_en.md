# **PART I — COMMON METHODOLOGICAL FRAMEWORK**

## **1.1 General introduction**

This document integrates municipal feasibility assessments for climate mitigation actions in the Waste, Transport, and Energy sectors, developed within the Municipal Mitigation Accelerator (MEED) by SSG and Open Earth Foundation. The three assessments share a common methodology; normative details, scores, and justifications are sector-specific and are presented in Parts II, III, and IV respectively.

The instrument evaluates whether the current legal framework enables a municipality to act, whether it has the institutional capacity required, and whether accessible financing sources exist. It is designed to be applied across Chile's 345 municipalities and produces weighted action- and sector-level scores.

The core analytical distinction in the instrument is the separation between normative competence — the legal authority enabling municipal action — and implementation instrument — the specific mechanism (ordinance, procurement terms, Local Regulatory Plan, agreement) through which that competence is exercised. Competence may exist without an active instrument, and vice versa; this distinction is essential for correctly scoring the Local Enabling Instrument sub-criterion within Local Governance.

## **1.2 Assessment objectives**

— Determine the overall state of municipal feasibility for climate mitigation actions in each sector.

— Evaluate and guide the improvement of municipal plans, programs, and investments in mitigation.

— Provide a national benchmarking tool to guide decisions by municipalities, SEREMIs, Regional Governments (GORE), and financing institutions.

— Identify legal, institutional, and financial gaps requiring central or regional intervention.

## **1.3 Assessment method — approach and deployment**

Instrument components are analyzed using an approach-and-deployment methodology. Response options reflect a normative, not empirical, choice that determines implementation and maturity level. Options represent discrete compliance levels: 100 (full compliance), 50 (partial compliance with conditions or risks), and 0 (non-compliance in the current legal and institutional context).

## **1.4 Application method**

Responses should be selected by officials familiar with the area, service, or issue being assessed. Options do not necessarily match each municipality's reality exactly; selection should reflect the closest fit to the municipality's current situation. Results should be public and integrated with other indicators such as FIGEM and FET to guide resource allocation. It is recommended that results be presented to the team involved in the process and shared with the Municipal Council.

## **1.5 Final score calculation**

The final score (0–100) is calculated as the weighted sum of three components:

— Legal Viability (VL): 40%

— Local Governance (GL): 30%

— Financing (F): 30%

**Final Score (PF) = Legal Viability x 40% + Local Governance x 30% + Financing x 30%**

Interpretation ranges:

| Range | Interpretation | Description |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| PF >= 75 | Viable action | Strong legal basis, sufficient institutional capacity, and accessible financing. Recommended for direct implementation. |
| 40 <= PF < 75 | Viable with conditions | Feasible but requires overcoming identified barriers. Possible with a roadmap and prior strengthening. |
| PF < 40 | Low viability | Significant barriers. Requires legal change, institutional development, or extraordinary financing. |

## **1.6 Assessment dimensions and sub-criteria**

**1.6.1 Legal Viability (40%)**

Assesses whether the current legal framework authorizes municipal implementation of the action. Internal formula:

**VL = Competence ownership x 60% + Legal restrictions x 30% + Alignment with national policy x 10%**

| Sub-criteria | Weight | 100 — Fully compliant | 50 — Partially compliant | 0 — Not compliant |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Competence ownership. Can the municipality act directly? | 60% | A sector law (Law 18.695 or another) explicitly assigns the function to the municipality. | Competence exists but is conditioned, ambiguous, or mediated by an enabling instrument: procurement process, ordinance, council agreement. | Competence belongs to another level of government. Municipality cannot act on its own. |
| Legal restrictions. Absence of legal barriers | 30% | No restrictions. No Comptroller ruling, SEIA, or additional enabling act required. | Moderate legal risk: prior enabling act may be needed, process before Comptroller may be required, or there are administrative challenge precedents. | There is prohibition, explicit restriction, or legal reform would be required. |
| Alignment with national policy: ENRO 2040, LMCC, MMA plans | 10% | Action is directly aligned with an active national goal or plan. | Alignment is partial or referential: thematic coherence without direct mandate. | No alignment with current national policy, or conflict with national regulation. |

**1.6.2 Local Governance (30%)**

Assesses the municipality's effective institutional capacity to implement the action. Internal formula:

**GL = Technical capacity x 35% + External actor dependency x 30% + Local instrument x 25% + Precedent x 10%**

| Sub-criteria | Weight | 100 — Fully compliant | 50 — Partially compliant | 0 — Not compliant |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Technical and institutional capacity | 35% | Municipality has in-house technical team and documented sector experience. | Limited capacity. Requires external technical assistance or institutional strengthening. | Most municipalities lack technical capacity to implement autonomously. |
| Dependency on external actors (inverse criterion) | 30% | Municipality can act autonomously. External coordination is low/manageable. | Requires significant coordination with actors outside municipal control: GORE, MMA, REP private systems, SEREMI. | Fragmented governance. Municipality cannot lead without key external actors. |
| Local enabling instrument | 25% | There is an active local instrument (ordinance, PLADECO environmental component, PACCC) institutionalizing the action. | Instrument is possible but requires drafting and Municipal Council approval. | No local instrument exists. Action would be fully discretionary and vulnerable to administration changes. |
| Precedent in other municipalities | 10% | At least one Chilean municipality has successful, evaluated, replicable implementation. | Pilot experiences exist in Chile without a consolidated model or impact evaluation. | No known precedent in Chile. |

**1.6.3 Financing (30%)**

Assesses whether accessible financing sources exist to cover action costs. Internal formula:

**F = Availability of funding source x 75% + Financing accessibility x 25%**

| Sub-criteria | Weight | 100 — Fully compliant | 50 — Partially compliant | 0 — Not compliant |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Availability of identified source | 75% | Explicit, accessible, sufficient source to cover real action cost. | Funds exist but are competitive, partially cover components, or require prior BIP. | No accessible specific fund. Financial gap is significant. |
| Financing accessibility | 25% | Municipality can self-finance (DL 3.063 tariffs, fines) or there is guaranteed direct transfer. | Competitive funds with known process and reasonable access probability. | Uncertain process, unattainable requirements, or investment exceeds typical municipal capacity. |

## **1.7 Cross-cutting methodological notes**

[1] Percentage weights (40/30/30) are a normative, not empirical, choice. They reflect prioritization of legal risk as an enabling condition relative to governance and financing. For municipality-specific use, weights should be recalibrated with local data; this instrument operates as national benchmarking.

[2] "Legal restrictions": in the absence of specific Comptroller rulings that validate or declare certain designs unlawful, risk judgment is prudential, based on legality principles and indirect jurisprudence. Each justification states when risk is interpretive and not confirmed by a firm ruling.

[3] The distinction between normative competence and implementation instrument is analytically essential: the Local Enabling Instrument sub-criterion scores existence (or possibility) of the concrete mechanism of exercise, not only existence of general competence.

[4] Identified legal gaps do not invalidate the instrument. They are explicitly recognized gray zones where legal risk is scored prudentially. The instrument adopts a conservative posture: absent firm ruling, strict legality applies.

[5] Local Governance and municipal heterogeneity: the Sanitation and Ornamental Services Unit (art. 25 Law 18.695) is mandatory only in municipalities with more than 100,000 inhabitants. In smaller municipalities, the function exists under art. 3(d) but without a formal unit. This difference is reflected in justifications when analytically relevant.

# **PART II — WASTE SECTOR**

## **2.1 Sector introduction**

The Waste sector instrument is the pilot component of the MEED methodology and serves as a template for Transport and Energy. It evaluates 11 climate mitigation actions related to household solid waste management, recycling, final disposal, and recovery.

Waste management is one of the few areas where Law 18.695 (LOCM) grants exclusive municipal competence (art. 3(d): sanitation and community ornamentation). However, this general competence coexists with specific sector regulations — especially REP Law 20.920, DL 3.063 on municipal revenues, and Law 21.100 on plastics — that define the actual municipal action space.

Reading of results: key municipal levers in this sector are service operation through procurement terms, waste tariff setting within DL 3.063 parameters, enforcement of national standards (Law 21.100, DS 12/2020), and construction-waste management through DOM in formal works. In landfills, wastewater biogas management, REP system operations, and larger-scale eco-parks, municipalities can only operate at the margins as coordinators/facilitators without own decision-making authority.

## **2.2 Municipal exercise mechanisms — Waste**

| Mechanism | Description | Examples / references |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Direct competence | Express legal mandate. Municipality acts without dependency on other actors. | icare_0064 — Law 18.695 art. 3(d) |
| Procurement terms | Municipal authority exercised when contracting services by imposing operator conditions. Without strong terms, competence weakens in practice. | c40_0035, c40_0037 — Law 18.695 art. 6 |
| Municipal ordinance | Local regulation and enforcement. Can reinforce national standards but cannot create obligations without explicit legal enabling. | c40_0040 (DL 3.063 arts. 6–9); c40_0034 (Law 21.100) |
| Regional coordination | Action exceeds municipal scale. Requires GORE, SEREMI, or other actor. Municipality cannot act autonomously. | c40_0036 (SEREMI Health, DS 189/2005); icare_0122 (SEREMI MMA, DS 8/2012 art. 5(d)) |

## **2.3 Relevant administrative/judicial jurisprudence**

Rulings by the Comptroller General (CGR) and Environmental Courts are primary legal sources for scoring legal risk by action. Identified gaps do not invalidate the instrument; they are gray zones where judgment is prudential and pending future firm jurisprudence.

## **2.4 Main legal references — Waste**

Core references include Law 18.695, DL 3.063, CGR rulings on waste charges, DS 189/2005, DS 4/2009 MINVU, REP framework (Law 20.920 + DS 12/2020), Exempt Resolution 9/2022 MMA, and ENRO 2040 / LMCC Law 21.455.

# **PART III — TRANSPORT SECTOR**

## **3.1 Sector introduction**

Transport feasibility assessment determines action-level feasibility based on legal, institutional, and financial criteria specific to the municipal territory. It is framed within PACCC obligations under Chile's Climate Change Framework Law (Law 21.455).

The transport analysis identifies a structural tension: many high-impact actions (concession fleet electrification, emissions standard regulation, congestion pricing) fall under MTT or GORE competences, not municipal authority. Actions with higher municipal viability are those operating on assets or spaces under direct municipal control.

A cross-cutting principle: alignment with national policy (LMCC, sector transport plans) does not transfer regulatory competence. A municipality may be aligned with national goals without authority to regulate the relevant system.

## **3.2 Municipal exercise mechanisms — Transport**

| Mechanism | Description | Examples / references |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Direct competence over own assets | Municipality acts over its own fleet, public spaces, and concessions without dependence on other government levels. | c40_0029; icare_0121 |
| Coordination with MTT/GORE | Main action belongs to MTT or GORE. Municipality acts on complementary infrastructure/planning spaces without decision authority over concessioned service. | c40_0023, icare_0117 |

# **PART IV — ENERGY SECTOR**

## **4.1 Sector introduction**

Energy feasibility is structured in two tracks reflecting different action types: Track 1 groups energy-efficiency actions in municipal buildings and equipment (typically direct municipal competence, higher viability); Track 2 covers generation, networks, and industry (typically shared, conditional, or absent municipal competence).

Key distinction: municipalities, as autonomous public-law corporations, are not part of the central state administration in the same sense as ministries. Practical consequences include voluntary rather than mandatory treatment of some energy-management standards and continued reliance on Law 18.695 art. 5 as the direct enabling basis for actions on municipal assets.

In residential housing retrofit contexts, municipalities depend on MINVU call openings and program conditions; without active call windows, implementation cannot proceed regardless of local will.

## **4.2 Municipal exercise mechanisms — Energy**

| Mechanism | Description | Examples / references |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Direct competence over own assets | Municipality installs systems in own buildings, lighting, and fleet without authorization from another level. | c40_0017, icare_0040, icare_0137, icare_0012 |
| Procurement criteria | Municipality includes EE standards in procurement terms for own goods/services/works. | icare_0120 |
| Incentive ordinance | Municipality promotes voluntary certifications/bioclimatic design through local incentives. | icare_0010, icare_0028 |
| Sponsoring/facilitator role | Municipality organizes demand and supports applications to MINVU/Ministry of Energy programs without control over call openings or subsidy allocation. | c40_0015, icare_0025 |
| No enabling / national competence | Action belongs to CNE/distributors/regulated entities; no municipal regulatory authority over electric market or private industrial processes. | icare_0008, icare_0105, icare_0023, icare_0026, icare_0009 |

## **4.3 Main legal references — Energy**

Includes Law 18.695, Law 21.305, Law 19.886, Law 20.936 + Law 21.185, DS 1/2020, DS 27 MINVU, LGUC/OGUC framework, DFL 382/1988, Law 18.902, Law 18.410, DFL 1/2005, and LMCC references.

## **4.4 Track 1: EE actions in municipal buildings and equipment**

Track 1 concentrates actions where municipal autonomy is highest. It contains the highest-energy-sector viability actions, including actions scoring 100. Lower-scoring actions in this track are those dependent on MINVU housing programs where the municipality acts as a Sponsoring Entity without control over program availability.

## **4.5 Track 2: Generation, networks, and industry actions**

Track 2 illustrates municipal limits as an energy actor. Regulation of electricity networks and markets is primarily national; municipalities have no regulatory/sanctioning authority over these systems. Positive viability in this track generally appears when municipalities act as local counterparts to national/rural programs or facilitators in specific contexts.

# **PART V — INSTRUMENT USE AND IMPLEMENTATION GUIDE**

## **5.1 How to read the instrument**

Total score (0–100) is obtained via `PF = VL x 40% + GL x 30% + F x 30%`.  
Each component is a weighted sum of sub-criteria scored in discrete levels (0, 50, 100). Intermediate values come from weighted formulas, not direct subjective scoring.

When legal risk is described as "prudential" or "interpretive," it indicates no firm Comptroller or judicial ruling confirms or rejects the position. In such cases, the instrument adopts a conservative strict-legality approach.

## **5.2 Instrument limitations**

— The instrument is national benchmarking: scores are comparative references, not absolute municipality-specific evaluations.

— Capacity/governance sub-criteria reflect the typical Chilean municipality profile and should be recalibrated with local data for municipality-specific applications.

— Identified legal gaps (absence of firm CGR/court rulings on specific designs) are risk signals requiring specialized legal management.

— The instrument assesses implementation feasibility, not mitigation impact.

## **5.3 Suggested application process**

— Stage 1 — Diagnosis: identify whether each action has (or can have) local enabling instruments (ordinance, PLADECO, PACCC, agreements).

— Stage 2 — Assessment: complete sub-criteria with competent municipal technical teams.

— Stage 3 — Prioritization: rank actions by total score and required effort to overcome barriers.

— Stage 4 — Roadmap: for "Viable with conditions" actions, define concrete barrier-resolution steps (develop local instrument, formulate BIP project, coordinate with GORE/SEREMI, apply to specific funding).

— Stage 5 — PACCC integration: include prioritized actions in the municipal climate action plan with goals, timelines, and responsible teams.

## **5.4 On the Financing dimension**

The Financing dimension distinguishes between direct-access mechanisms (municipality is direct recipient) and competitive/conditional mechanisms (access depends on application, territorial prioritization, or BIP formulation). A fund may exist without being practically accessible at a given time. Frequently cited funds include FNDR, MMA FPA, DS 7/2017 MMA Recycling Fund, Ministry of Energy programs, PMU SUBDERE, CORFO programs, PER, and programs from the Energy Sustainability Agency.
