# **1. The problem: one national score across 345 different realities**

The MEED instrument produces a single score per mitigation action that functions as a national benchmark: it reflects the typical feasibility of an average Chilean municipality for each action. That score is useful for comparing actions with each other and for guiding policy decisions at the central level. But it has a structural limitation that this document states explicitly: the same score of "Conditional" or "Viable" means radically different things depending on the municipality reading it.

Chile has 345 municipalities ranging from large urban municipalities with in-house technical teams, a strong Municipal Works Directorate (DOM), SECPLA with urban planners, and autonomous investment budgets, to rural municipalities of 2,000 inhabitants where the municipality is effectively the mayor, the municipal secretary, and four or five staff members. Between those extremes there is a heterogeneous distribution that no single score can fully capture.

This note does not modify the MEED instrument scores. What it does is propose a differentiated reading of those scores by municipal typology, using two analytical variables: SUBDERE's municipal classification and the Municipal Common Fund (FCM) as an indicator of fiscal autonomy and implementation capacity.

## **1.1 Why heterogeneity matters beyond population size**

The first intuition when thinking about municipal diversity is size: large municipalities have more capacity than small municipalities. That is true on average, but population size only partially captures reality. There are at least three additional dimensions that determine the effective feasibility of a mitigation action:

— Fiscal autonomy: the share of own-source revenues over total revenues determines whether the municipality can commit resources without depending on external competitive funds. A municipality with high FCM dependency cannot co-finance investments or sustain recurring operating expenses not covered by transfers.

— Installed technical capacity: the existence of specialized units (SECPLA with urban planners, Environmental Unit, Traffic Directorate with professional staff) determines whether the municipality can formulate BIP projects, design technically sound bidding terms, or manage complex contracts. This capacity does not perfectly correlate with size.

— Territorial context: rurality, urban density, and local economic base determine which actions are physically possible. A bike-sharing program (icare_0121) requires a minimum urban density that does not exist in rural municipalities. A composting system (icare_0064) may be easier in small municipalities with lower waste volumes than in large ones with complex logistics.

## **2.2 What changes in effective feasibility by bracket**

The MEED instrument scores three dimensions per action: Legal Viability (VL), Local Governance (GL), and Financing (F). Of these three, Local Governance is the dimension that varies most across brackets, particularly in the sub-criteria Technical and Institutional Capacity and Financing Accessibility. Legal Viability is in principle the same for all municipalities (the law is the same), although the capacity to exercise it is not.

The analytical hypotheses this document proposes for empirical validation are:

— Hypothesis 1 — Technical capacity gap: the Technical and Institutional Capacity sub-criterion (weighted at 35% of GL, which itself weighs 30% of the total) is calibrated in the MEED instrument for the average municipality situation. In Bracket 1 this sub-criterion should rise to 100 for most instrument actions; in Bracket 4 it should drop to 0 for all actions requiring specialized teams.

— Hypothesis 2 — Financing gap: the Financing Accessibility sub-criterion scores 50 when funds are competitive with a reasonable probability of access. That probability is not the same across brackets: the ability to formulate BIP projects is a condition for real access to FNDR, and that capacity is practically null in Bracket 4 without external technical assistance.

— Hypothesis 3 — Gap inversion for low-cost actions: for actions with zero or near-zero operating cost (icare_0120 — EE criteria in procurement; c40_0034 — enforcement of Law 21.100), the gap between brackets is inverted or disappears. The decision to include EE criteria in procurement terms does not require specialized technical capacity or additional budget.

— Hypothesis 4 — Differential relevance of FCM: for actions requiring initial capital investment (c40_0029 — fleet electrification; icare_0012 — solar generation), FCM as a share of total budget is the most relevant fiscal determinant, more than population size.

## **3.1 Actions whose viability is robust across all brackets**

A first expected result of calibration is that a subset of MEED instrument actions will maintain high viability (>= 75) across all municipal brackets. These are actions that simultaneously meet three conditions: (i) legal competence is unequivocal and does not require specialized technical capacity to be exercised; (ii) implementation cost is null or very low; and (iii) financing does not depend on competition or complex technical project formulation.

The provisional candidate actions for this category are: icare_0120 (EE criteria in procurement), c40_0034 (enforcement of Law 21.100), and icare_0137 (LED replacement) in its most basic version. These actions should be the starting point of any municipal roadmap regardless of bracket.

## **3.2 Actions with high inter-bracket gap**

At the other extreme, a subset of actions will show significant gaps: viable in Bracket 1 and low viability in Brackets 3-4. These are actions combining high technical requirements, significant initial capital investment, and dependence on project formulation capacity. Provisional candidates are: c40_0037 (separate collection), icare_0122 (eco-parks), c40_0029 (fleet electrification at relevant fleet scale), and icare_0012 (solar generation with complex contractual management).

For these actions, the MEED instrument should be complemented with an explicit recommendation: the "Conditional" or "Viable" score applies mainly to Bracket 1 and partially to Bracket 2; for Brackets 3 and 4, effective viability requires sustained external technical assistance or pooled implementation with other municipalities.

## **3.3 Special case: pooled (inter-municipal) actions**

Some actions that are unviable for an individual Bracket 3-4 municipality may become viable if implemented jointly through municipal associations or in coordination with GORE. This applies especially to: separate collection systems (c40_0037), eco-parks (icare_0122), inter-municipal bike sharing (icare_0121), and regional-scale composting systems (icare_0064 in its larger-scale version).

The MEED instrument does not evaluate pooled-action viability; it evaluates individual municipality viability. This is a limitation that could be addressed in a future version of the instrument with a "pooled-scale viability" module that evaluates additional governance and financing requirements for inter-municipal cooperation.

# **4. The Municipal Common Fund as a fiscal reading variable**

The Municipal Common Fund (FCM) is the main fiscal redistribution mechanism among Chilean municipalities. Its relevance for the MEED instrument is not as a scoring criterion — that path was already discarded in previous versions because including it created analytical confusion — but as an external reading variable that helps interpret Financing-dimension scores.

| FCM dimension | Effect on effective feasibility | Implication for the MEED instrument |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Transfer amount (high FCM = high dependency) | Municipalities with FCM > 70% of revenues have no budget margin to co-finance actions requiring own investment. The Financing score assumes competitive funds are accessible; in B4, BIP project formulation capacity is the real bottleneck. | The "Financing accessibility" sub-criterion should be differentiated: in municipalities with high FCM dependency, score 50 (competitive with reasonable probability) is optimistic without external technical assistance. |
| FCM formula (distribution factors) | FCM is distributed by poverty, territorial extension, number of exempt properties, and other factors. Municipalities with high rurality and low tax base receive more FCM per capita but with lower spending management capacity. The paradox is that the most dependent municipalities are also those that most need the instrument as a prioritization guide. | FCM is not a simple capacity proxy: there are small municipalities with high FCM and efficient management (e.g., mining municipalities with royalty) and medium municipalities with low FCM but low real autonomy. SUBDERE classification is more precise than FCM alone. |
| Interannual FCM variability | FCM varies according to national revenue and local factors. Budget uncertainty especially affects actions requiring multi-year commitments (LED lighting contracts, bike-sharing concessions, electric fleets with leasing). | Actions with recurring operating cost (icare_0122, c40_0037) are more vulnerable than actions with low recurring cost (icare_0120, c40_0034). ## **4.1 What FCM does not capture**

FCM is not a perfect proxy for municipal capacity. There are three situations that distort interpretation if FCM is used as the only indicator:

— Mining municipalities or those with high-yield taxable economic activities: they may have low FCM (net contributors) but very high own-source revenues. Their effective viability is high despite receiving little FCM.

— Municipalities with relevant municipal assets (land, concessions, water rights): they may have co-financing capacity not reflected in FCM.

— Municipalities with efficient management of competitive funds: some small municipalities have project teams that systematically access FNDR, PMU, and other funds, compensating for low fiscal autonomy. FCM does not capture this management capacity.

For these reasons, the recommendation is to use FCM in combination with SUBDERE typology, not as a standalone variable.

## **4.2 Implications for the instrument's Financing dimension**

The Financing Accessibility sub-criterion (25% weight within F, which weights 30% of total) scores 50 when funds are competitive with a reasonable probability of access. The hypothesis this document proposes for validation is that this reasonable probability is not uniform across brackets:

— In Bracket 1: probability of access to competitive funds (FNDR, FPA, PMU) is effectively medium-high because the municipality has a project team. Score 50 is accurate.

— In Brackets 3-4 without technical assistance: probability of access is low because BIP project formulation requires technical capacity the municipality does not have. Score 50 overestimates real accessibility. In these brackets, score should be 0 for funds requiring BIP projects, and 50 only for direct-access funds or simplified formulation.

If empirically validated, this adjustment would imply that several Financing scores in the MEED instrument are overestimated for lower brackets, especially for actions such as c40_0035 (route optimization, requires BIP), icare_0122 (eco-parks, requires BIP and SEIA), and icare_0006 (microgrids, requires specialized technical formulation).

# **5. Law 21.074 on Regionalization Strengthening: current status and projection**

Law 21.074 (2018) created the mechanism for transfer of competencies from the central level to Regional Governments (GORE), operationalized through transfer agreements. It is important to distinguish two things that are sometimes conflated in public debate: Law 21.074 transfers competencies from the central level to GORE, not to municipalities. Municipalities remain dependent on GORE as intermediary, and their position in the governance chain does not automatically change.

| Dimension | Current status (pilot) | Potential implication for the MEED instrument |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Competencies transferred with resources | As of the instrument date, Law 21.074 has not materialized transfers with resources in the evaluated sectors (waste, transport, energy). The pilot mechanism is operational but effective transfers are concentrated in other areas. | The instrument reflects the current state. Actions classified as "regional coordination" or "low viability" due to GORE dependency are not yet modified by this law. |
| Potential effect on regional-coordination actions | If GORE were to receive competencies in regional-scale waste management (landfills, eco-parks), regional transport, or rural electrification, the municipality role as local counterpart would strengthen. But the flow is central -> GORE, not central -> municipality directly. | Actions such as c40_0036 (landfills), icare_0049 (regional RE), and icare_0122 (eco-parks) could move from "no municipal authority" to "coordination with strengthened GORE." Requires monitoring of effective transfers. |
| GORE Infrastructure and Transport Division (DFL 1/2005 art. 68 e)) | Already existed before Law 21.074. GORE has powers in regional transport and territorial planning that the instrument already includes (ipcc_0001, icare_0121 at metropolitan scale). | This is currently the most relevant channel. Law 21.074 may strengthen those powers but has not yet substantially expanded them in the instrument sectors. |

## **5.1 Why this matters for the MEED instrument**

The MEED instrument classifies several actions as "regional coordination" or "low viability" precisely because competency belongs to an actor outside municipal control: MTT, CNE, SEREMI of Health, or GORE. If GORE receives transferred competencies in any of these areas, the municipality's situation as GORE interlocutor may improve, but it does not automatically become municipal competency.

The most plausible effect of Law 21.074 on the MEED instrument, if relevant transfers materialize, would be in two types of actions:

— Regional-scale actions currently dependent on GORE: c40_0036 (landfill upgrades), icare_0049 (regional RE centers), icare_0122 (scale eco-parks). If GORE receives competencies in regional-scale waste management or energy infrastructure development, its coordinator role is strengthened and municipalities gain an interlocutor with greater authority. Local Governance score could increase in the External Actor Dependency sub-criterion.

— Territorial planning (ipcc_0001, ipcc_0074): Law 21.074 already strengthened GORE competencies in territorial ordering (PROT, PRC consistency). This is already incorporated in the instrument, which cites DFL 1/2005 and Law 21.074 as part of the ipcc_0001 framework.

## **5.2 Monitoring recommendation**

Given the pilot nature of transfers under Law 21.074, the recommendation is to establish an annual review process for the MEED instrument that specifically monitors:

— Competencies effectively transferred to GORE through agreements in waste, transport, and energy sectors.

— Resources associated with those transfers: a competency without resources does not modify effective viability.

— Effects on the municipality-GORE relationship in the instrument's regional-coordination actions.

If in a future review effective transfers with resources are confirmed in any of these areas, scores for affected actions should be recalibrated, specifically the External Actor Dependency sub-criterion in the Local Governance dimension.

# **6. Recommendations for differentiated use of the MEED instrument**

## **6.1 For SSG and Open Earth Foundation**

— Do not present MEED scores as uniform feasibility thresholds for all municipalities. Always accompany results with the warning that the instrument operates as national benchmarking and effective feasibility varies by SUBDERE bracket.

— Prioritize empirical validation of this note's hypotheses with a sample of municipalities from each bracket before scaling the instrument to all 345 municipalities.

— Consider developing a bracket-adjustment module that automatically recalibrates Technical and Institutional Capacity and Financing Accessibility sub-criteria based on the municipality's SUBDERE classification.

## **6.2 For presentation to municipalities**

— For Brackets 1 and 2: the MEED instrument can be used directly as a prioritization guide. Scores are reasonably accurate for these municipalities.

— For Brackets 3 and 4: the instrument should be presented with the warning that actions classified as "Viable" or "Conditional" assume technical and institutional capacity that may not be available. The roadmap for these municipalities should begin with actions robust across all brackets (icare_0120, c40_0034, icare_0137) and scale progressively with external technical assistance.

— For any bracket: actions classified as "Low viability" should not be automatically discarded if the municipality is part of a municipal association or has access to technical assistance from SUBDERE, GORE, or sector programs.
